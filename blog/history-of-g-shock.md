In 1981, watches were highly fragile instruments. Dropping a quartz timepiece onto a hard surface meant shattering the crystal, snapping the movement, and essentially destroying the device. 

Enter Casio engineer Kikuo Ibe. 

After accidentally dropping and breaking a pocket watch given to him by his father, Ibe formed a three-person task force internally known as **Project Team Tough**. Their singular goal was to build an unbreakable watch.

## The "Triple 10" Specification
The team set an absurd engineering constraint known as the "Triple 10" criteria:
*   **10-year** battery life
*   **10-bar** (100 meters) water resistance
*   Survival of a **10-meter** drop onto a hard surface

## The Breakthrough: Hollow Structure
Initially, the team tried wrapping the watch module in layers of dense rubber. The problem? To absorb enough kinetic energy to survive a 10-meter drop, the rubber shell had to be the size of an apple, rendering the watch completely unwearable.

The breakthrough came to Ibe while watching a child bounce a rubber ball in a park. He realized that the center of the ball suffered almost no shock upon impact. 

Instead of wrapping the watch in thick armor, Ibe designed a **hollow structure**. The core quartz timekeeping module was suspended inside the outer case, supported by only a few crucial points of contact. The module was essentially floating in a void, completely isolated from direct external impacts.

## The DW-5000C (1983)
After dropping over 200 prototypes out of a third-story bathroom window at the Casio R&D center, the team finally succeeded. In 1983, they released the **DW-5000C**, the very first G-Shock.
